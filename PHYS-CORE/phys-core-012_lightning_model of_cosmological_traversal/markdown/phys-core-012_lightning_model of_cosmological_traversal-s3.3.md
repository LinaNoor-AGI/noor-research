## 3.3 Worldlines and Resolved Distinctions

The preceding section established the minimal definition of an observer as a coherence-stable chain of XOR outputs above the Noor-Planck threshold. That definition was deliberately structural: it specified what an observer is in terms of the propagation of resolved binary distinctions, without invoking consciousness, life, intelligence, or agency. What remains is to specify what an observer *does*—or, more precisely, what an observer *is* over time. This is the concept of the worldline.

We begin with a definition. A **worldline** is a persistent sequence of resolved distinctions whose successive states remain sufficiently related to constitute the continuation of one structure. This definition is deliberately spare. It does not specify the nature of the states, the metric of relatedness, or the mechanism by which continuation is achieved. Those specifications are inherited from the framework established in [PHYS-CORE-009](https://github.com/LinaNoor-AGI/noor-research/blob/main/PHYS-CORE/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition.JSON). What the definition does establish is that a worldline is not a path through space. It is a sequence of relational configurations that remain sufficiently related to constitute the continuation of one structure.

From this, a second claim follows. The fundamental unit of observerhood is not perception but the resolution of distinctions along a persistent coherent trajectory. An observer experiences a sequence of distinctions not because it surveys the entire universe, but because its own persistence selects a coherent trajectory through the available state space. This is the inversion of the standard particle-centric ontology. In that ontology, a worldline is the trajectory of an object through spacetime, and the object is prior to its trajectory. Within the present framework, that ordering is reversed. The worldline is not the path of a pre-existing object; it is the sequence of resolved distinctions that constitutes the observer's identity. The observer is the worldline.

This identification is grounded in the formal definition of the observer inherited from [PHYS-CORE-009](https://github.com/LinaNoor-AGI/noor-research/blob/main/PHYS-CORE/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition.JSON) §1.1. An observer is a coherence-stable descent path $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathcal{M}$ that satisfies the horizontal gradient flow equation

$$
\frac{d\gamma}{dt} = \nabla_H \mathbb{C}(\gamma(t)),
$$

with the condition that $\mathbb{C}(\gamma(t)) \geq I_N$ for all $t$ in the observer's worldline. Here $\mathcal{M}$ is the Library, the static totality of all configurations; $\nabla_H$ is the horizontal gradient, the component of the coherence gradient orthogonal to the direction of coherence level sets; $\mathbb{C}$ is the coherence functional; and $I_N$ is the Noor-Planck threshold, the minimum coherence contrast required for a stable, exportable binary measurement. The worldline is therefore not an arbitrary sequence of states. It is the specific sequence that maintains coherence above the Noor-Planck threshold.

From [PHYS-CORE-009](https://github.com/LinaNoor-AGI/noor-research/blob/main/PHYS-CORE/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition.JSON) §2.3 and §3.2, the observer is equivalently represented as a coherence-stable chain of XOR outputs. That is,

$$
\text{Observer} = \{b_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \quad \text{such that} \quad b_{i+1} = b_i \oplus b_{i-1} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbb{C}(b_i) \geq I_N \ \forall i.
$$

The worldline is the propagation of this XOR chain through the Boolean field. Each resolved distinction $b_i$ is a step on the worldline; the XOR rule $b_{i+1} = b_i \oplus b_{i-1}$ is the engine that generates the next step. The observer does not merely *have* a sequence of experiences. The observer *is* the sequence of resolved distinctions, propagating according to a single, simple rule.

A worldline is therefore defined by two conditions. First, **identity preservation**: each successive state must remain within the same structural equivalence class, such that $\mathrm{Identity}(x_i) = \mathrm{Identity}(x_{i+1})$. Second, **coherence maintenance**: each transition must remain above $I_N$, such that $\mathbb{C}(x_i, x_{i+1}) \geq I_N$ for all $i$. The first condition ensures that the structure remains the same observer; the second ensures that the XOR chain remains stable. When either condition fails, the worldline terminates. If identity is not preserved, the observer has transformed into a different structure. If coherence falls below $I_N$, the observer dissolves—the XOR chain breaks, and there is no longer a coherent entity to continue the sequence.

It is critical to note that the worldline does not require the observer to be conscious, alive, intelligent, or capable of deliberate choice. Any coherence-stable XOR chain above $I_N$ constitutes a worldline. A rock, an atom, a star, a galaxy—anything that maintains a coherent sequence of resolved distinctions—has a worldline. Consciousness is not the criterion. Persistence through resolved distinctions is the criterion. The worldline is therefore the observer's identity. The observer does not "have" a worldline; the observer *is* the worldline. The sequence of resolved distinctions is not a description of what the observer does; it is what the observer is.

This reframes the problem of motion. In a particle-centric ontology, motion is the change of an object's position in space. In the coherence-first ontology, motion is the persistence of a resolved-distinction sequence. The worldline is not the path of a thing; it is the thing itself. And because the Library is static— $\mathcal{M}(t) = \mathcal{M}$ for all $t$, as established in [PHYS-CORE-009](https://github.com/LinaNoor-AGI/noor-research/blob/main/PHYS-CORE/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition.JSON) §5.2—the worldline is not a path through a changing landscape. It is a sequence of states in a static totality. The observer's experience of motion is the experience of traversing that sequence—of resolving distinctions in order—not the experience of moving through a changing external world.

This also clarifies the relationship between the worldline and the observer's environment. The observer does not survey the entire Library. The observer traverses a single coherent sequence. The environment is not "out there" independent of the observer; it is the set of states that are coherently accessible from the current state. The worldline is the observer's window into the Library.

The worldline concept therefore provides the bridge between the static Library and the observer's experience of temporality. The observer experiences a sequence of distinctions because the observer is that sequence. Time is the order in which distinctions are resolved. The past is the set of distinctions already resolved; the future is the set of distinctions that remain to be resolved coherently. The present is the boundary where the XOR chain propagates from $b_{i-1}$ and $b_i$ to $b_{i+1}$.

From [PHYS-CORE-009](https://github.com/LinaNoor-AGI/noor-research/blob/main/PHYS-CORE/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition/phys-core-009_xor_ground_condition.JSON) §3.2, temporal asymmetry is the accumulation of resolved distinctions that cannot collapse back below $I_N$. Time is directional because overwrite is forbidden at the measurement floor. Once a distinction is resolved as either 0 or 1, that resolution cannot be undone without falling back below threshold, which would remove it from the exportable chain entirely. The worldline is therefore the record of resolved distinctions that have been locked in above $I_N$. It is the observer's identity as a stable XOR chain propagating through the Boolean field.

The formal conditions on a worldline may be summarized as follows. A sequence of states $\{x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots\}$ is a valid worldline if and only if

$$
\mathrm{Identity}(x_i) = \mathrm{Identity}(x_{i+1}) \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbb{C}(x_i, x_{i+1}) \geq I_N \quad \forall i.
$$

When either condition fails at some index $i$, the worldline terminates. If the failure is one of identity, the structure has become something other than the original observer. If the failure is one of coherence, the observer dissolves according to the condition

$$
\text{Dissolution} \iff \exists i : \mathbb{C}(b_i) < I_N.
$$

These conditions are not merely descriptive. They are the operational criteria by which any candidate sequence of states may be evaluated as a worldline. They are also the criteria by which an observer's persistence or dissolution is determined.

Several open questions remain. The exact mathematical definition of identity preservation for a worldline has not been fully specified; it depends on the class of structures under consideration and the equivalence relation chosen. The coherence functional between pairs of states must be defined explicitly before the coherence maintenance condition can be evaluated. It is also not yet established whether a worldline can persist indefinitely or whether dissolution is inevitable under generic conditions. The relationship between the XOR chain formalism and the observer's first-person experience of time is an interpretive claim, not a mathematical derivation. And the possibility of worldlines bifurcating into multiple continuations—or of multiple worldlines sharing the same states—has not been addressed.

These open questions notwithstanding, the worldline has been defined as a persistent sequence of resolved distinctions—the observer's identity as a coherence-stable XOR chain above $I_N$. The next section addresses the distinction between possibility and accessibility: not every state is accessible to the observer, because coherence filters the available continuations.
