## 2.2 Distinction Without Privilege

XOR establishes distinction without assigning ontological priority to either operand. The operation is symmetric: exchanging the operands does not change the result. Thus, distinction is produced by the relation between two configurations, not by a privileged status belonging to one of them.

### Symmetry of distinction

For any configurations \(a\) and \(b\) in the formal domain \(D\), XOR satisfies the commutative identity

$$a \oplus b = b \oplus a.$$

*The result is unchanged when the operands are exchanged. If* \(a\) *differs from* \(b\), *then* \(b\) *differs from* \(a\) *to exactly the same degree represented by the XOR operation. In the Boolean case used here, this is expressed by*

$$a \oplus b = 1 \Longleftrightarrow b \oplus a = 1.$$

*The operation therefore represents difference without specifying a direction from one operand toward the other. The distinction exists at the relational level: it is the fact that* \(a\) *and* \(b\) *are not identical under the operation, rather than a property that XOR assigns independently to either operand.*

### Distinction is relational

XOR does not produce a distinction by declaring one configuration to be primary and the other to be secondary. It evaluates the relation between the two operands. In particular, the operation contains no term that selects \(a\) as ontologically prior to \(b\), or \(b\) as ontologically prior to \(a\).

$$a \oplus b = b \oplus a.$$

**Formal consequence.** There is therefore no operand-selection rule within the primitive operation itself. Any later interpretation that treats one configuration as an observer, reference point, origin, or privileged frame must be introduced by a subsequent construction rather than inherited from XOR.

### Why the absence of privilege matters

This symmetry provides the first structural reason that NSFG does not require a privileged observer at its axiomatic floor. The primitive operation supplies distinction, but it does not supply a distinguished position from which all distinctions must be evaluated. No configuration is selected by XOR itself as the universal reference against which every other configuration must be measured.

This does not yet establish observerhood, observer-relative resolution, or coherence. Those are downstream constructions. The narrower point established here is that the primitive operation does not contain an asymmetry from which a privileged observer could be derived. When observerhood is subsequently defined, it must therefore arise from structural properties of configurations and their persistent distinguishability rather than from an a priori privileged status.

### Formal Statement

**Symmetry.** $a \oplus b = b \oplus a$

**Distinction equivalence.** $a \oplus b = 1 \Longleftrightarrow b \oplus a = 1$

**No operand privilege.** The operation contains no term selecting $a$ as ontologically primary over $b$.

**Derived boundary.** Therefore, no privileged observer is supplied by the XOR primitive itself.

### Derived vs. Interpretive

**Formally derived**

- XOR is commutative.
- Exchanging the operands leaves the XOR result unchanged.
- The distinction represented by XOR does not depend on assigning ontological priority to either operand.
- The primitive operation contains no operand-selection rule.

**Interpretive**

- The symmetry is foundationally useful for avoiding a privileged observer.
- Any privileged observer framework would require an additional construction or axiom not present in the axiomatic floor.

---

A symmetric operation can be repeatedly applied. The mathematical result of allowing that operation to close over its domain is the Library.
