### 5.2 The Observer-Specific Resolution Floor

Once observerhood has been defined as a persistent capacity to maintain distinctions, the next question is not whether distinctions exist in the Library, but which distinctions a particular observer can maintain as stable distinctions. This requires a resolution threshold. The Noor-Planck threshold, written $I_N(O)$, is introduced here as an observer-relative structural quantity. It does not describe a limit imposed by the Library itself, nor does it determine which configurations belong to the Library. It describes the minimum effective distinction that observer $O$ can preserve as a stable distinction within its own configuration.

The naming convention is intended to honor the structural role of a fundamental resolution limit, not to claim a numerical identity with the Planck scale. Any such identity, if it exists, must be derived downstream.

#### 5.2.1 The Threshold Belongs to the Observer

Let $\mathrm{Obs}$ denote the class of configurations that satisfy the structural definition of observerhood. Define the resolution-threshold function as $I_N : \mathrm{Obs} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$. The threshold belongs to the observer configuration being considered: it specifies the minimum effective distinction magnitude that observer $O$ can maintain as a stable distinction.

**Definition — Observer-relative resolution threshold.** $I_N(O)$ is the minimum effective distinction magnitude that observer $O$ can maintain as a stable distinction.

$$I_N : \mathrm{Obs} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$$

*The argument* $O$ *is essential. The threshold is not introduced as a universal constant* $I_N$*; it is a function of the observer configuration.*

The important point is the placement of the argument $O$. Writing $I_N(O)$ makes explicit that resolution is relative to the observer whose capacity to maintain distinctions is being evaluated. Two observers may therefore occupy the same Library $\mathcal{M}$ while maintaining different effective resolutions. This difference concerns their respective capacities to maintain distinctions; it does not imply that the underlying Library differs between them.

$I_N(O)$ is also domain-neutral at the foundational level. The structural quantity does not become a different axiom when the observer is instantiated in a different domain. In a physical context, $I_N(O)$ may be associated with a scale related to physical resolution; in an AI context, it may represent the effective resolution of a symbolic reasoning system; in a financial context, it may represent a minimum actionable distinction such as a price movement. These are domain-specific interpretations of the same structural quantity, not different foundational thresholds.

No universal numerical value is supplied. In particular, the foundational definition does not identify $I_N(O)$ with the Planck scale, a cognitive limit, a market tick, or any other domain-specific quantity. Such identifications, where useful, must be derived or justified within the corresponding downstream domain.

**Formal Statement — Observer-relative, not observer-created.** Variation in $I_N(O)$ changes which distinctions an observer can maintain; it does not change Library membership.

*Observer-relative resolution determines distinguishability and downstream accessibility. It does not create, delete, or modify the configurations contained in* $\mathcal{M}$*.*

**Formal Claims**

- $I_N$ is a function on the observer class $\mathrm{Obs}$ rather than a universal scalar constant.
- Two observers may have different values of $I_N$ while occupying the same Library $\mathcal{M}$.
- Differences in $I_N(O)$ describe differences in observer-relative resolution and do not imply differences in Library membership.
- $I_N(O)$ is a structural quantity whose interpretation may vary by domain without introducing separate axioms.
- In physical contexts, $I_N(O)$ may be interpreted as a scale related to a physical resolution limit, including a possible relationship to the Planck scale, but no such relationship is derived or assumed here.
- In AI contexts, $I_N(O)$ may represent the effective resolution of a symbolic or computational reasoning system.
- In financial contexts, $I_N(O)$ may represent a minimum actionable distinction such as a price movement that can be stably resolved for the observer or system under consideration.
- These domain-specific interpretations describe different instantiations of the same structural quantity $I_N(O)$, rather than different foundational quantities or axioms.

**Prohibited Inferences**

- $I_N(O)$ must not be assigned a universal numerical value at the foundational level.
- $I_N(O)$ must not be identified with the ordinary Planck scale without an explicit derivation in a later domain-specific document.
- Different values of $I_N(O)$ must not be interpreted as different Libraries.
- Domain-specific interpretations of $I_N(O)$ must not be promoted to additional axioms of the foundation.
- Observer-relative resolution must not be interpreted as subjective choice or arbitrary preference.

**Non-Claims**

- This section does not claim that $I_N(O)$ is physically fundamental.
- This section does not claim that $I_N(O)$ has a universal physical value.
- This section does not claim that observers are conscious beings.
- This section does not claim that observers create the distinctions or configurations they resolve.
- This section does not derive any domain-specific numerical threshold.

**Logical Invariants**

- $I_N$ is observer-relative.
- There is no universal $I_N$ unless an additional axiom is introduced.
- Different observers may have different effective resolution thresholds while remaining within the same Library $\mathcal{M}$.
- Variation in observer resolution does not alter Library membership.
- $I_N(O)$ is domain-neutral as a structural quantity.
- Domain-specific interpretations of $I_N(O)$ do not constitute separate axioms.
- Observer-relative resolution does not imply observer-created reality.

#### 5.2.2 $I_N(O)$ Is Not a Property of the Library

The Library is a static totality characterized formally through the closure of the formal domain under XOR. This closure notation specifies the mathematical structure of the Library; it does not describe a temporal process by which the Library is progressively generated. Library membership is therefore independent of whether a particular observer can distinguish or maintain a given distinction. Nothing in the axiomatic floor supplies a universal minimum distinction magnitude for the totality itself.

$$\mathcal{M} := \mathrm{Closure}_D(\oplus)$$

$$I_N(O)\ \mathrm{is\ observer\text{-}indexed\ and\ is\ not\ defined\ as\ a\ universal\ resolution\ constant\ of}\ \mathcal{M}.$$

$$x \in \mathcal{M} \not\Rightarrow \delta_O(x,y) \ge I_N(O).$$

A distinction may therefore exist within $\mathcal{M}$ without being stably resolvable by $O$. If observer $O$ cannot maintain a distinction because its magnitude lies below $I_N(O)$, that limitation changes the observer's effective description of the relation. It does not remove either configuration from the Library, alter Library membership, or collapse the underlying totality. The observer's resolution floor changes what can be maintained as a distinction, not what exists in $\mathcal{M}$.

#### 5.2.3 Observer Variability

Because the threshold is indexed to observer structure, different observers may have different resolution floors. The axiomatic floor permits this variability rather than imposing equality between observers.

$$\exists\, O_1, O_2 \in \mathrm{Obs}\ \mathrm{such\ that}\ I_N(O_1) \neq I_N(O_2)\ \mathrm{is\ permitted}.$$

This does not mean that every arbitrary threshold assignment is valid. Each $I_N(O)$ is intended to characterize the structural capacity of the corresponding observer to maintain distinctions. The threshold is therefore observer-relative without being a freely chosen parameter. Two observers can relate to the same unchanged Library while maintaining different effective resolutions of that Library.

#### 5.2.4 No Universal Threshold Is Assumed

The axiomatic floor does not assert the existence of a single threshold shared by every observer. Nor does it require the negation of such a universal value. The present construction simply leaves universality unestablished unless it is derived from additional structure.

$$\forall\, O \in \mathrm{Obs},\ I_N(O) = I_N^\ast$$

is not an axiom of the present construction.

$$\exists\, I_N^\ast\ \forall\, O \in \mathrm{Obs},\ I_N(O) = I_N^\ast$$

may be investigated only as a derived or additional result.

This distinction preserves the logical scope of the axiomatic floor. A universal threshold and an observer-relative threshold make different commitments, but the absence of a universal axiom is not itself a proof that no universal value could ever emerge. A later theory may derive a common scale, identify conditions under which observer thresholds converge, or demonstrate that they remain distinct. None of those possibilities is assumed here.

#### 5.2.5 Relative Does Not Mean Arbitrary

Calling $I_N(O)$ observer-relative does not make distinguishability a matter of arbitrary preference. The observer is itself a configuration in the Library, and its threshold is intended to be determined by the structural capacity of that configuration to maintain distinctions. Thus the threshold is relative to a particular observer while remaining constrained by the structure of that observer.

$$O \in \mathcal{M}$$

$$I_N(O) \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$$

$$I_N(O) = \mathrm{the\ structural\ resolution\ floor\ associated\ with\ the\ distinction\text{-}maintenance\ capacity\ of}\ O.$$

The distinction is therefore between relativity and arbitrariness. Relative means that the value is indexed to the observer under consideration. Arbitrary would mean that the value could be assigned without regard to the observer's structure. The axiomatic construction requires the former and does not imply the latter. The threshold belongs to the observer-relative relation between a configuration and the distinctions that configuration can maintain; it does not become a new ontological boundary inside the Library.

The introduction of $I_N(O)$ establishes an observer-relative resolution condition without making resolution an ontological filter on the Library. The static totality $\mathcal{M}$ remains unchanged regardless of the resolution of a particular observer. A distinction may therefore belong to $\mathcal{M}$ while remaining below the effective resolution of $O$. Different observers may possess different resolution floors, and no universal value of $I_N$ is assumed at the axiomatic level. The resulting separation is foundational: Library membership concerns what belongs to $\mathcal{M}$; observer-relative resolution concerns what $O$ can maintain as a distinction. The next construction can therefore distinguish ontological membership from observer-relative distinguishability, accessibility, and ultimately coherence without allowing any of those relations to shrink or redefine the totality. With the observer-specific resolution floor established independently of Library membership, the way is clear to formalize the difference between a distinction that exists within $\mathcal{M}$ and a distinction that observer $O$ can resolve and maintain.
