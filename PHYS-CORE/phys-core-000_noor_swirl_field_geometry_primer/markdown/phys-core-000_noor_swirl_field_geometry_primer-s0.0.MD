## 0. How to Read the Primer

This primer should be read as a dependency chain rather than as a collection of assumptions about the physical universe. Its purpose is to establish the smallest formal starting point from which the later constructions of Noor Swirl Field Geometry can be developed. The discipline is therefore subtractive before it is constructive: concepts that have not yet been established must not be silently imported into earlier definitions. At the same time, the reader must distinguish between the formal machinery used to characterize the Library and any interpretation of that machinery as an ontological generation process. The Library is treated here as a static, maximally complete totality. XOR and closure specify its formal structure; they do not describe a temporal process by which the Library comes into existence.

**Axiom.** *An axiom is a primitive statement admitted at the starting point of the formal system rather than derived from an earlier statement within that system.* The axiomatic floor contains exactly one primitive axiom.

$$A_N = \{A_\oplus\}$$

$$A_\oplus: \oplus \text{ is the primitive operation of distinction.}$$

The word "distinction" gives the intended interpretation of the primitive operation. It does not introduce a second mathematical primitive.

**Definition.** *A definition introduces notation or specifies the meaning of a construction in terms of concepts that have already been admitted.* The Library is not an axiom. It is formally characterized using the closure of the primitive operation over a minimal formal domain, together with the maximal-static-totality invariant established at the foundational level.

$$\mathcal{M} := \mathrm{Closure}_D(\oplus)$$

The distinction is important: closure is a mathematical characterization of the configuration structure of $\mathcal{M}$, not a temporal generation mechanism. The primer does not describe a process in which a smaller Library exists first and subsequently grows into a larger one. The Library is taken as a single static totality, while closure specifies the formal relation that characterizes its membership structure.

**Mathematical Construction.** *A mathematical construction is an object obtained by applying definitions and ordinary mathematical operations to structures already admitted by the formal system.* Examples include:

- $\mathcal{M} := \mathrm{Closure}_D(\oplus)$
- relations defined on elements of $\mathcal{M}$
- sequences of configurations
- observer-relative distinguishability relations
- threshold functions
- observer-relative accessible subsets
- continuation spaces
- observer-relative graphs

A construction may be as mathematically rich as necessary, but it does not become a new primitive merely because it is useful or foundational to later sections. Likewise, a mathematical closure operation must not be interpreted as an ontological history unless such an interpretation is explicitly introduced at a later layer.

**Invariant.** *An invariant is a condition that is maintained throughout the formal interpretation rather than being generated anew by traversal.* The Library is a single static, maximally complete totality. Its maximality is not introduced merely to make the configuration domain sufficiently large. It prevents any configuration or worldline from being silently privileged as the universal origin of coherent traversal.

$$\forall i,\ \mathcal{M}_i = \mathcal{M}$$

The notation $\mathcal{M}_i = \mathcal{M}$ is used only to express invariance under traversal. It does not imply that multiple temporal copies of the Library exist. At every observer-relative traversal step, the underlying Library remains the same totality. No configuration or worldline is designated as the unique universal starting point from which all other configurations must be constructed. An admissible configuration may instead serve as an observer-relative reference without changing the Library itself. The observer is relationally situated with respect to the whole Library rather than being ontologically localized to a temporally generated neighborhood within it. Membership in $\mathcal{M}$ is not conditioned on coherence, observability, accessibility, persistence, physicality, relevance, or apparent temporal location.

**Consequence.** *A consequence is a statement that follows from the axiom together with definitions, stated invariants, and ordinary mathematical inference.* The notation $A_N \vdash P$ means that proposition $P$ is derivable from the stated axiom set together with the definitions, explicit foundational invariants, and ordinary mathematical inference permitted by the formal system.

A consequence must not be presented as though it were independently assumed. Conversely, an interpretation must not be presented as though it were formally derived when additional empirical or physical assumptions would be required. The maximal-static-totality condition must not be described as a theorem that follows from XOR alone. It is a foundational structural requirement of the NSFG interpretation, motivated by the absence of a privileged universal worldline or reference. Likewise, the requirement that every admissible reference configuration possess a coherent continuation is a later derived requirement under the model's assumptions and must not be attributed to maximality alone.

**Interpretation.** *An interpretation assigns conceptual or physical meaning to a formal construction without changing the formal construction itself.* Examples include:

- Interpreting a persistent distinguishable configuration as observer-like.
- Interpreting repeated survivorship as an analogue of law-like regularity.
- Interpreting an observer-relative accessible graph as a possible foundation for later relational geometry.
- Interpreting traversal ordering as the source of an observer-relative past/future distinction without assigning an intrinsic temporal partition to the Library.

Interpretation is downstream of formal construction. The primer therefore distinguishes what the mathematics states from what the mathematics may later be used to represent. In particular, observer-relative temporal ordering must not be mistaken for temporal evolution of the Library itself.

**Minimal Assumptions.** The purpose of the primer is to minimize assumptions rather than maximize them. A one-primitive ontology is not made stronger by introducing more primitives. Each additional primitive carries its own assumptions and therefore increases the amount of structure that must be accepted before derivation begins. The intended method is instead to establish the smallest formal floor that can support the subsequent constructions. The primer therefore does not begin with physical space, physical time, causality, matter, energy, consciousness, information as a substance, or a privileged observer.

Not assuming a concept at the axiomatic floor does not mean that the concept is rejected. It means that the concept must earn its place later through definition, derivation, or explicitly stated interpretation. Minimal assumptions do not require a progressively generated Library. The formal floor may characterize a maximal static totality without introducing a temporal process by which that totality is constructed.

**Single Primitive.** XOR is the only primitive.

$$A_\oplus: \oplus \text{ is the primitive operation of distinction.}$$

The operation $\oplus$ is admitted before the later structures are introduced. The formal domain on which it operates is kept deliberately minimal and is not identified with a physical ontology. Every later construction must therefore either be defined from the primitive and the mathematical structures required to manipulate it, or be explicitly identified as a later interpretive or physical extension.

**Library.** The Library is formally characterized by XOR closure and treated ontologically as a static, maximally complete totality.

$$\mathcal{M} := \mathrm{Closure}_D(\oplus)$$

The Library is formally characterized after a minimal formal domain $D$ has been specified. Closure specifies the mathematical closure structure associated with the primitive operation. It does not describe a temporal sequence in which the Library is first absent, then partially generated, and finally complete. The Library is treated as a single static totality whose maximal completeness is maintained throughout observer-relative traversal.

The Library is maximally complete because no configuration or worldline is privileged as the universal origin of traversal. Any admissible configuration may serve as an observer-relative reference point without changing the Library itself. The Library does not evolve from simple to complex. It is maximally complete at every abstract traversal step: $\forall i,\ \mathcal{M}_i = \mathcal{M}$.

The observer's state, history, resolution, and current relational reference may change. Those changes modify structures defined over $\mathcal{M}$, such as accessibility and coherent continuation, but they do not modify membership in $\mathcal{M}$. Past and future are not primitive subdivisions of $\mathcal{M}$. They arise as ordering relations within observer traversal.

At this stage, membership in $\mathcal{M}$ does not imply persistence, coherence, physicality, observability, or accessibility. Conversely, inaccessibility does not imply non-membership in $\mathcal{M}$.

**Observer and Reference.** Observer-relative structures are relations over the unchanged Library, not mechanisms that create or locally delimit the Library. The term "observer" will later denote a configuration possessing a specified structural property. It does not initially mean a human, a conscious mind, an agent, or a privileged point of view.

An admissible configuration may serve as an observer-relative reference configuration $x$. The reference determines the observer's current relational evaluation of continuation; it does not determine which configurations belong to $\mathcal{M}$. The observer is relationally situated with respect to the whole Library while maintaining a restricted coherent continuation relation. This distinction must be preserved throughout the primer.

Observer-relative resolution determines which distinctions can be maintained by a particular observer. It therefore affects distinguishability, coherence, and accessibility without becoming a universal criterion for Library membership.

**Dependency Discipline.** Later concepts must not be smuggled into earlier definitions. The reader should continuously ask what a statement depends upon. If a definition of an early object requires a concept that has only been introduced later, the dependency chain has been reversed. Such a reversal can make a theory appear to derive a concept when the concept was actually assumed from the beginning.

- Do not define XOR using physical events when physical events have not yet been introduced.
- Do not define the Library as a physical container.
- Do not define the Library as a process that grows from a simpler state into a more complex state.
- Do not define an observer as a conscious entity.
- Do not use physical time to define persistence before temporal structure has been established.
- Do not define coherence as an intrinsic property of a path before an observer-relative resolution threshold has been defined.
- Do not interpret accessible reality as the entirety of ontological existence.
- Do not treat an observer's changing relation to the Library as evidence that the Library itself has changed.
- Do not treat past and future as pre-existing ontological regions of the Library.

For every major statement, the reader should be able to ask: "Was this assumed, defined, derived, required as an invariant, or interpreted?"

**Physical Language.** Conventional physical language is deferred until a formal construction can support a physical interpretation.

*Space.* Not primitive. The formal domain $D$ is not physical space. It is introduced only as the minimal mathematical substrate required for $\oplus$ to have operands.

*Time.* Not primitive. When sequences are first introduced, their indices represent abstract ordering rather than physical time. A sequence such as $\gamma = (x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ therefore describes formal ordering without presupposing a physical temporal dimension. The resulting ordering belongs to traversal; it does not partition the Library into intrinsic past and future regions.

*Causality.* Not primitive. An ordered relation between configurations must not automatically be interpreted as physical causation. Any causal interpretation belongs to a later layer.

*Observer.* Structural rather than psychological. The term "observer" will later denote a configuration with a specified structural property. It does not initially mean a human, a conscious mind, an agent, or a privileged point of view.

*Complexity.* Not an intrinsic evolution of the Library. Any apparent increase in complexity belongs to the observer's experienced relational structure. The Library itself does not progress from simple to complex.

**Formal versus Interpretive.** The formal layer contains the primitive operation, mathematical definitions, closure characterization, static-totality invariant, relations, sequences, threshold functions, subsets, and logical consequences. The interpretive layer asks what those formal structures might mean when related to concepts such as observation, reality, lawfulness, geometry, traversal, or navigation. The two layers may inform one another, but they must not be conflated. An interpretation cannot silently become an axiom, and a formal closure relation cannot silently become an ontological generation history.

**Reading Protocol.**

1. *Identify the formal object being introduced.* What mathematical object is being defined?
2. *Identify its dependencies.* Which previously established objects are required for this definition?
3. *Classify the statement.* Is it an axiom, definition, construction, invariant, consequence, requirement, or interpretation?
4. *Check for imported physical assumptions.* Has physical space, time, causality, matter, energy, consciousness, or a privileged observer been introduced prematurely?
5. *Separate Library membership from observer-relative access.* Does the statement concern membership in the static formal Library, or only what a particular observer can resolve or traverse coherently?
6. *Check for hidden temporal generation.* Does the wording imply that the Library itself is being constructed, evolving, or becoming more complete?
7. *Check reference neutrality.* Does the statement secretly privilege one configuration or worldline as the universal origin of traversal?
8. *Follow the dependency chain forward.* What is the next construction that becomes possible once the present definition or invariant has been established?

**Scope Boundary.** The following are included at this stage:

- A single primitive operation.
- A minimal formal domain.
- A formal closure characterization of the Library.
- The Library as a static, maximally complete totality.
- The absence of a privileged universal worldline or reference.
- Mathematical definitions and constructions derived from that floor.
- A strict separation between formal derivation and interpretation.
- The distinction between the Library, observer state/history, and observer-relative structures defined over the Library.

The following are not yet included:

- Physical spacetime.
- Physical laws.
- Matter.
- Energy.
- Consciousness.
- AI cognition.
- Cosmology.
- Specific field equations.
- A primitive past/future partition of the Library.
- A physical interpretation of traversal ordering.

These concepts may become subjects of later NSFG work, but introducing them here would obscure whether they are consequences of the axiomatic floor or assumptions imported into it.

- The formal domain $D$ is a minimal mathematical substrate and is not physical space.
- The Library is formally characterized by XOR closure.
- Closure is not a temporal generation process.
- The Library is a static, maximally complete totality.

- The Library does not progress from simple to complex.

- The observer is relationally situated with respect to the whole Library.
- Library membership is not conditioned on coherence, observability, accessibility, persistence, physicality, relevance, or apparent temporal location.
- A mathematical construction does not become a primitive merely because it is foundational to later sections.
- A consequence must not be presented as an independent assumption.
- An interpretation must not be presented as a formal derivation.
